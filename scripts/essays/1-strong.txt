Every Thursday my grandfather and I argue about the correct way to fold a dumpling. He pinches eleven pleats, left to right, the way his mother did in Harbin. I pinch nine, because nine is what fits between the edge of my thumb and the tip of my index finger, and because I was eight when I decided that eleven was showing off.

For years I thought the argument was about dumplings. It was the only conversation we could have without a translator. My Mandarin stalled at the level of a polite third grader, and his English stopped at "good job" and "careful." So we talked with our hands. When the filling was too wet, he tapped the bowl twice. When my pleats leaned, he turned my wrist a few degrees without a word. I learned the recipe the way you learn a song in a language you do not speak: perfectly, and without knowing what any of it meant.

Last spring he had a stroke that took most of the strength out of his right hand. The first Thursday after he came home, I set out the flour and the pork and the little dish of water, and he watched me fold eleven pleats. I had never done it before. He tapped the table twice, which I had only ever seen him do to a bowl, and I realized I had no idea what it meant.

So I asked. Badly, in Mandarin, with my phone translating the words I did not have. It took us most of an hour to get through one sentence. He said the tapping was what his mother did when she wanted him to slow down and look. It was never about the filling. It was an instruction to pay attention.

Since then I have started recording him. Not the recipe, which I already know, but the words around it: the name of the street where his mother sold dumplings in winter, the reason he left, the Chinese word for the smell of wet flour that apparently has no English equivalent. I have forty-one recordings and a spreadsheet of vocabulary that my Mandarin tutor calls "extremely specific to pork." My speaking has improved more in six months than in six years of Saturday school, because for the first time I need the language for something only it can do.

I used to think fluency meant knowing enough words to never be misunderstood. Now I think it means being willing to be misunderstood for an hour to get one sentence right. I still fold nine pleats when he is not looking. But I have learned to tap the table twice when my little sister rushes, and she has started asking what it means.
