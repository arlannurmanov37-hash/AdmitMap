When I joined my school's robotics team freshman year, I did not know how to use a drill. I had signed up because my best friend did, and I expected to spend the season handing people parts. Instead, I ended up discovering what I want to study in college.

Our team was small, only nine members, and our mentor believed in giving everyone real responsibility. In my first week he handed me the drivetrain and told me to figure out why the robot kept drifting to the left. I spent three afternoons measuring wheels, checking motors and reading forum posts before I found the problem: one gearbox had been assembled with a spacer missing. It was a tiny mistake, but finding it gave me a feeling I had never had in a classroom. The problem was real, and nobody knew the answer until I did.

Over the next three years I became the team's mechanical lead. I learned CAD, taught younger members how to wire motor controllers, and spent more weekends in our shop than at home. The hardest moment came during my junior season, when our robot failed inspection the morning of the regional competition. Our intake was two inches too wide. We had ninety minutes to fix it. I remember everyone looking at me, and I remember being terrified. We cut the frame, re-drilled the mounting holes and passed inspection with eleven minutes to spare. We finished fourth, our best result ever.

Robotics taught me that I like problems that have a physical answer. It also taught me that leadership is mostly staying calm when other people are nervous. I want to study mechanical engineering because I want to keep solving problems like the missing spacer, problems that seem small but decide whether something works. I am excited to bring the same persistence I learned in our shop to college.
